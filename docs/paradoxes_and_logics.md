Let's explore a range of paradoxes and the types of logic or philosophical approaches that have been proposed to address them. 

1. Fuzzy Logic (as we've discussed):
   - Sorites Paradox (heap)
   - Baldness Paradox
   - Ship of Theseus (identity over time)

2. Many-Valued Logic:
   - Liar Paradox: "This sentence is false."
   - Truth-teller Paradox: "This sentence is true."
   - Curry's Paradox: "If this sentence is true, then Santa Claus exists."

3. Paraconsistent Logic:
   - Russell's Paradox: "The set of all sets that do not contain themselves"
   - Barber Paradox: "The barber shaves all and only those who do not shave themselves"

4. Temporal Logic:
   - Grandfather Paradox (time travel)
   - Newcomb's Paradox (decision theory and determinism)

5. Deontic Logic (logic of obligations and permissions):
   - Contrary-to-Duty Paradox: Dealing with conflicting obligations
   - Good Samaritan Paradox: "It ought to be that Jones helps Smith who has been robbed"

6. Epistemic Logic (logic of knowledge and belief):
   - Surprise Examination Paradox
   - Knowability Paradox: "All truths are knowable" leads to "All truths are known"

7. Quantum Logic:
   - Schrödinger's Cat Paradox
   - Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen (EPR) Paradox

8. Intuitionistic Logic:
   - Brouwer's Continuity Theorem (challenging the law of excluded middle)

9. Relevance Logic:
   - Paradoxes of Material Implication: "If the moon is made of green cheese, then 2+2=4"

10. Free Logic:
    - Paradoxes involving non-existent objects: "The present king of France is bald"

11. Infinitary Logic:
    - Galileo's Paradox (about infinite sets)
    - Hilbert's Hotel Paradox

12. Modal Logic:
    - Fitch's Paradox of Knowability
    - Modal Paradox of Buridan's Bridge

Special concepts associated with specific logics:

1. Vague Predicates (Fuzzy Logic):
   - Tall, short, rich, poor, near, far
   - Color boundaries (e.g., when does orange become red?)

2. Paradoxical Sentences (Many-Valued Logic):
   - Self-referential statements
   - Circular definitions

3. Inconsistent Concepts (Paraconsistent Logic):
   - Naive set theory
   - Inconsistent legal or moral codes

4. Temporal Concepts (Temporal Logic):
   - "Now", "then", "always", "sometimes"
   - Causality and determinism

5. Normative Concepts (Deontic Logic):
   - Obligation, permission, prohibition
   - Moral dilemmas

6. Epistemic Concepts (Epistemic Logic):
   - Knowledge, belief, certainty, doubt
   - Common knowledge in game theory

7. Quantum Concepts (Quantum Logic):
   - Superposition, entanglement, measurement

8. Constructive Concepts (Intuitionistic Logic):
   - Provability, constructive existence

9. Relevant Implication (Relevance Logic):
   - Causation, explanation

10. Existence and Reference (Free Logic):
    - Fictional entities, future individuals

11. Infinite Concepts (Infinitary Logic):
    - Transfinite numbers, infinite sequences

12. Possibility and Necessity (Modal Logic):
    - Possible worlds, counterfactuals

Each of these paradoxes and associated concepts presents unique challenges and opportunities for our Logic Fusion system. They demonstrate the need for a flexible, multi-faceted approach to logic that can handle a wide range of reasoning patterns and edge cases. By incorporating these diverse logical frameworks, we can create a more robust and versatile system capable of addressing complex real-world reasoning scenarios.
